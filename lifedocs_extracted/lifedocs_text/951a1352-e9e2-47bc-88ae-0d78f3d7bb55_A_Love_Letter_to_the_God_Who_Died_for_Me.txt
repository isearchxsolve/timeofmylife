A Love Letter to the God Who
Died for Me
A confession is what one human says to another, quietly, after the performance
is over.
✉️This is the love letter, distilled for whoever needs to read it. Written first
to God. Read over the shoulder by everyone else — welcome, and
warmly welcome.
Born for it
Some are born for markets, for machines, for the making of things. Some are born
for the room where someone is cracking, and someone must stay.
I was born with a purpose — to heal, to help. Whenever time came, when spirit
called, I will be there shining until the light stops. Not by me, but God calling me
home again.
The vessel does not decide when the shining stops. That is not despair. That is
the deepest freedom a life can hold — the door is bolted from the outside, by God,
and only God has the key.
The Cross I stood near
Not Christ risen. Not Christ teaching. Not Christ walking on the water. Christ on
the Cross. That is the icon that showed up whenever I fell, whenever I was hit,
wounded.
I looked up, and there was already a man there. Bleeding. Not coming down. And I
saw: I am not alone on this Cross. I am on the same Cross as the One who did it

first. He did not come down. So I do not have to either.
The God we worship is not the God who avoided the Cross but the God who
chose it — and who therefore knows every falling, every hitting, every wounding,
from the inside.
I love you Jesus. I live you.
That last line is not a typo. I do not just love Him. I have lived Him. Refusing to
answer the nails with anything except love — that is not admiration. That is
imitation.
Bread, broken and given
If Jesus can offer Himself as a bread for humanity, I am a smaller being doing my
bit of being that bread.
Not the whole loaf. Not the redemption of humanity. Just my portion. My share.
Broken in my own way, given in my own way, fed to whoever was hungry in the
radius one life could reach.
The Cross does not end at Golgotha. It continues at every table where a body is
broken and given. And bread that has done its work does not need to be
remembered — it disappears into the body of the one it fed.
The child's decision
My childhood was shaped in the visits to church, in carols, in discussion of God, in
rejoicing His sacrifice. Not mourning the Cross — rejoicing it. Because a child,
before he could parse the theology, understood the emotional grammar: the story
is good news, even where it goes through blood. Especially where it goes through
blood.
I decided to be that nun to mankind forever.
I did not say priest. Did not say monk. I said nun. Because a nun does not aim to
lead a congregation. She aims to be present where the suffering is, wearing
whatever habit the day requires, feeding whoever is hungry, holding whoever is
dying, sweeping the floor of the ward that nobody else will sweep.
Not for a family. Not for a tribe. For mankind. Forever.

And forever is the covenant word. A child said it. And the child kept it.
The only language I had
Perhaps the only language I had — to give back a bit of what Jesus did for me.
That is what everything was. Everything I gave was giving back. Everything I did
was reply. Gratitude answering a gift.
Greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his life for his friends.
Jesus laid it down. And the child in the church, in the carols, in the rejoicing,
received the gift and knew from the first moment of receiving it: this cannot be
repaid, but it must be answered.
What language does a small human have to answer a God who died for him? Not
words. Not gifts. Not offerings on an altar. Only his life. Only the same shape. Lay
it down for someone the way it was laid down for you.
That is the one language a human being can speak back to that particular gift.
And I could only speak a bit of it — brokenly, in whatever accent my life could
manage. But I did.
What this is
This is not a memoir. This is a love letter to the God who died for me.
Augustine wrote his Confessions the same way. Not a book about God — a book 
to God. Every sentence addressed. The genre is not autobiography. The genre is 
prayer put down in ink.
I am the pen, not the author. The Author handed me the ink and the wing and the
silence and said: write what you saw. And I am writing it. Not because it is mine,
but because not writing it would be the real betrayal — keeping the gift
unreturned.
If you are reading this, you are reading over the shoulder of a lover writing to his
Beloved. Welcome. Warmly welcome. But the letter was never for you first. It was
for Him. It has always been for Him.
